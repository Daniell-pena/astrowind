# What to Do When You Can't Sleep at Night: A Real Action Plan That Works

You're staring at the ceiling, your brain is running at full speed, and sleep feels completely out of reach. It happens to most people at some point, but knowing what to actually do in that moment, rather than just lying there getting more frustrated, makes a real difference. This guide covers both the immediate moves you can make tonight and the longer-term habits that stop the problem from coming back.

**Short answer:** When you can't sleep at night, get out of bed after 20 minutes of wakefulness, go to a dim room, and do something calm and non-stimulating until you feel drowsy. Avoid bright light and screens. Keep your wake time consistent the next morning regardless of how little sleep you got. This approach, backed by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, is a core part of cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).

---

## Why You Can't Sleep Right Now (And Why It Matters)

Before you can fix the problem, it helps to know what is actually happening. The two most common causes of nighttime wakefulness are a racing or anxious mind and a body that is not physically ready for sleep.

Your body runs on something called sleep pressure, which builds throughout the day the longer you stay awake. If that pressure is low, maybe because you napped late or went to bed too early, your brain simply has no biological reason to sleep yet.

The second driver is arousal. When your nervous system is activated, whether from stress, blue light, caffeine, or even the frustration of not sleeping, your cortisol rises and signals your brain to stay alert. Lying in bed struggling actually trains your brain to associate the bed with wakefulness, which is exactly the opposite of what you want.

Understanding this matters because the right action depends on which problem you have. A body clock problem needs a different fix than an anxious mind problem.

## What Should You Do If You Can't Sleep After 20 Minutes?

The single most well-supported strategy from sleep medicine is called stimulus control. The rule is simple: if you have been lying awake for around 20 minutes, get out of bed.

Do not watch the clock obsessively. Use a rough estimate. The point is not precision, it is breaking the habit of lying in bed awake.

Once out of bed, go somewhere dim and do something low-stimulation. Options that work well include:

- Reading a physical book under soft, warm light
- Light stretching or slow yoga
- Listening to calm audio, like a podcast on a topic you find mildly interesting but not gripping
- Writing down whatever is on your mind to clear your mental buffer

Avoid your phone, bright overhead lights, work tasks, and anything that pulls your attention hard. When you feel genuinely sleepy, go back to bed.

## Does Getting Up Really Help, or Does It Make Things Worse?

This is one of the most common worries people have. The idea of leaving a warm bed feels counterproductive. But the Sleep Foundation and AASM both describe stimulus control as one of the most effective behavioral interventions for insomnia, and it has a solid track record across clinical research.

The logic is straightforward. Every minute you spend lying awake in bed reinforces a mental link between bed and wakefulness. Getting up breaks that association over time. It feels uncomfortable at first, especially in week one, but most people find it starts working within two to three weeks of consistent use.

A few honest caveats. If you have a chronic pain condition or mobility issues, leaving bed repeatedly may not be realistic. In that case, lying still with eyes closed in a dark room while listening to calming audio is a reasonable substitute. Also, if wakefulness is happening every single night for more than a month, it is worth talking to a doctor or sleep specialist, because CBT-I with a trained therapist outperforms self-directed approaches for chronic insomnia.

## Which Relaxation Techniques Actually Work When You Can't Sleep?

There are several evidence-informed techniques worth having in your toolkit. Not all of them work equally well for every person, so it makes sense to try a few and see what clicks.

**Slow diaphragmatic breathing** is the most accessible option. Breathing in for four counts, holding for four, and exhaling for six to eight counts activates your parasympathetic nervous system and physically reduces arousal. Harvard Health lists controlled breathing as one of the more reliable non-drug ways to reduce nighttime anxiety.

**Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR)** involves tensing and releasing muscle groups from your feet upward. It shifts attention away from racing thoughts and into the body. The NIH has documented PMR's effectiveness for sleep in multiple review papers.

**Body scan meditation** is similar. You move your attention slowly through different parts of the body without trying to change anything. It is a technique used widely in mindfulness-based stress reduction programs.

A few techniques that are popular but have weaker evidence include counting sheep (too simple to hold attention for most people) and listening to nature sounds (helpful for some, neutral for others). That said, if something works for you personally, the mechanism matters less than the result.

One thing that consistently does not help: trying harder to sleep. Effort creates arousal. Paradoxical intention, a technique where you try to stay awake while lying still in the dark, sometimes works better for anxious sleepers because it removes the pressure.

## What Should You Avoid Doing When You Can't Sleep?

Some of the most instinctive responses to wakefulness actually make things worse. Here is what to skip.

**Looking at your phone.** Even with a blue light filter on, the mental engagement of scrolling keeps your brain active. The content itself, news, social media, email, is often stimulating in a way that delays sleep significantly.

**Eating a large snack.** A light, low-sugar snack is fine if hunger is genuinely keeping you awake. But eating a real meal raises your core body temperature and signals your digestive system to activate, both of which work against sleep.

**Checking the clock repeatedly.** This is a major driver of sleep anxiety. Knowing it is 3 AM and you have four hours left adds pressure that makes sleep harder. If you can, turn clocks away from view.

**Taking a caffeine hit if you plan to sleep again.** This one comes up more than you would expect, especially with people who work night shifts. Even half a cup of coffee at 3 AM can push your next sleep window back by several hours. Check our [Caffeine Cutoff Calculator](/caffeine-calculator) to see how caffeine timing affects your specific situation.

**Alcohol as a sleep aid.** Alcohol helps you fall asleep faster but fragments sleep in the second half of the night. The NIH notes that alcohol suppresses REM sleep and increases awakenings. It tends to make next-day fatigue worse, not better.

## When Should You Think About Sleep Supplements or Tools?

If behavioral strategies alone are not enough, some supplements and sleep tools are worth considering. This is not medical advice, and you should talk to your doctor before starting any supplement, especially if you take medications or have a health condition.

**Melatonin** is most useful for circadian rhythm issues, like jet lag or shift work, rather than general insomnia. A low dose (0.5 mg to 1 mg) taken 30 to 60 minutes before your target sleep time is generally recommended by the Sleep Foundation. Higher doses are not more effective and may cause grogginess the next day.

**Magnesium glycinate** is a popular option that many people find supportive for relaxation. Evidence is still building, but it is generally well-tolerated. People with kidney issues should avoid supplemental magnesium without medical guidance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is it better to stay in bed and rest or get up when you can't sleep?**
If you have been lying awake for around 20 minutes, sleep medicine generally recommends getting up and doing something calm in dim light. Staying in bed frustrated can deepen the association between your bed and wakefulness, making the problem harder to fix over time.

**Q: What is the fastest way to fall asleep when your mind won't stop?**
Slow breathing, specifically a long exhale (try a 4-count inhale and 6 to 8 count exhale), activates your parasympathetic nervous system and reduces arousal quickly. Pair it with a body scan or progressive muscle relaxation to redirect attention away from anxious thoughts.

**Q: Can't sleep at night but sleepy during the day. What is going on?**
This pattern often signals misaligned sleep timing, fragmented nighttime sleep, or excessive time in bed relative to actual sleep need. It can also be a sign of sleep apnea, especially if snoring or gasping occurs. A sleep doctor can rule out underlying conditions if this is a consistent pattern.

**Q: How many nights of bad sleep before it becomes a problem worth addressing?**
Most sleep researchers define chronic insomnia as difficulty sleeping three or more nights per week for three or more months. Occasional bad nights are normal and rarely need intervention beyond good sleep habits. If you are hitting that three-month threshold, talking to a doctor or sleep therapist is a smart move.

**Q: Do sleep sounds or white noise actually help you fall back asleep?**
For many people, yes. White noise, pink noise, and brown noise work by masking inconsistent environmental sounds that cause brief arousals. The evidence is modest but consistent. ## The Bottom Line

When you can't sleep at night, the best immediate move is to stop fighting it. Get out of bed after 20 minutes, do something calm in dim light, and go back only when you feel genuinely sleepy. Avoid screens, alcohol, and clock-watching. Over the longer term, consistent wake times and lower-pressure habits around bedtime do more than any supplement or gadget. If the problem is persistent, CBT-I with a trained therapist is the most effective non-drug treatment available.
