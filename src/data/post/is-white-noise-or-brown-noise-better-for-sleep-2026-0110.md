# Is White Noise or Brown Noise Better for Sleep?

You've probably heard that white noise helps people sleep. But lately, brown noise has been getting a lot of attention, and for good reason. People who found white noise too sharp or grating are reporting much better results with brown noise's deeper, fuller sound. So which one actually works better for sleep, and does it depend on who you are?

**Short answer:** For most adults, brown noise is better for sleep because its lower-frequency, rumbling tone feels less harsh on the ears and is easier to sustain through the night. White noise works well for light sleepers who need strong, consistent sound masking. The best choice depends on your sensitivity to high-frequency sounds and the type of environment you're trying to block out.

---

## What Is the Difference Between White Noise and Brown Noise?

These two sound types look similar on the surface - both are steady, non-melodic sounds used for masking - but they feel very different to the ear.

**White noise** contains equal energy across all audible frequencies. Think of the static hiss from an old TV or a fan running at high speed. Because it includes high frequencies at full intensity, many people describe it as sharp, thin, or even slightly fatiguing over long periods.

**Brown noise** (sometimes called Brownian noise or red noise) has much more energy in the lower frequencies and rolls off sharply at higher ones. The result is a deep, rumbling sound, similar to a strong waterfall, distant thunder, or a large fan on a low setting. It feels "warmer" and less aggressive than white noise.

The name "brown" doesn't refer to color the way "white" does in white noise theory. It actually comes from Robert Brown, the scientist behind Brownian motion, which describes the random pattern that generates this type of sound signal.

Pink noise sits between the two, balancing energy across octaves. But in 2026, brown noise has overtaken pink noise in popularity among people seeking a calmer nighttime environment. ## What Does the Science Actually Say?

Here's the honest answer: the research on noise for sleep is promising but still developing. No large-scale, gold-standard trial has declared one color of noise the definitive winner for sleep.

What the science does support is **sound masking** in general. The Sleep Foundation notes that consistent background noise can reduce the contrast between quiet background sounds and sudden disruptive ones, which is what actually wakes most people up. It's the spike in sound level, not the overall volume, that interrupts sleep.

Studies referenced by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) have found that continuous broadband noise, including white and pink noise, can help people fall asleep faster and sleep more deeply in noisy environments. Brown noise hasn't been studied as directly, but its acoustic properties suggest it would work similarly for masking while potentially being more comfortable for long-term listening.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) hasn't issued a formal recommendation on noise color but does acknowledge that sleep environment management, including sound control, is a legitimate part of sleep hygiene.

The practical takeaway: both white and brown noise work via the same masking principle. If white noise is comfortable for you, it works. If it feels harsh, brown noise is the logical next step.

## Who Should Use Brown Noise vs White Noise?

The choice isn't just about preference. Your specific sleep problem matters a lot.

**Brown noise tends to work better for:**
- People who find white noise shrill, tinny, or harsh
- Adults with tinnitus or sensitivity to high-pitched sounds
- Anyone sharing a bedroom who wants a less intrusive background sound
- People trying to mask low-frequency disturbances like traffic, bass from neighbors, or HVAC rumble

**White noise tends to work better for:**
- Light sleepers who need maximum masking across all frequencies
- Infants and young children, where pediatric white noise machines are commonly used (at safe volumes)
- People in very noisy apartments where a wide frequency spread is needed to drown out varied sounds
- Shift workers who need to sleep during the day with significant ambient noise

If you're not sure which category you fall into, the [Sleep Quiz](/sleep-quiz) can help you identify your sleep environment challenges and point you toward the right tools.

One important note: this is general information, not medical advice. If you have a sleep disorder, hearing sensitivity, tinnitus, or are considering sleep aids for a child, talk to a healthcare provider before making changes.

## Does Brown Noise Help with Focus and Anxiety at Bedtime?

This is one of the main reasons brown noise has surged in popularity. Many people report using it not just during sleep but during the wind-down period before bed.

The deep, low rumble of brown noise seems to quiet mental chatter more effectively for some people than white noise does. Anecdotally, people with ADHD have been especially vocal about brown noise helping them focus and relax, though formal clinical research on this specific use is still limited.

From a sleep hygiene perspective, this matters. A calmer pre-sleep mental state leads to faster sleep onset, as the Sleep Foundation and Harvard Health both emphasize when discussing cognitive arousal as a barrier to sleep.

Using brown noise during a 20-30 minute wind-down routine, paired with reduced lighting and consistent timing, fits naturally into evidence-based sleep habits. For a full look at building that kind of routine, see [Sleep Habits and Routines That Work](/sleep-habits-and-routines-that-work).

The caution here: don't use brown noise as a substitute for addressing genuine anxiety or stress. If racing thoughts are disrupting your sleep regularly, that's worth discussing with a doctor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is brown noise safe to use every night?**
Yes, brown noise is generally safe for regular nightly use at moderate volumes. The main risk with any sleep sound machine is volume level. Keep it at a conversational level or below, roughly 50 to 65 decibels, and position it away from your head rather than directly beside your ear.

**Q: Can white noise or brown noise make sleep worse for some people?**
Yes, for some people any background noise adds stimulation rather than reducing it. If you find yourself focusing on the sound rather than relaxing, or if you feel more alert with it on, it's not the right tool for you. Silence, combined with other sleep environment improvements, may work better.

**Q: Does brown noise help with tinnitus?**
Many people with tinnitus report that brown noise, with its lower-frequency emphasis, helps mask the ringing more comfortably than white noise. However, this is not medical treatment. If tinnitus is affecting your sleep consistently, speak with an audiologist or ENT specialist about sound therapy options.

**Q: What volume should I play white or brown noise at for sleep?**
The AASM suggests keeping sleep sound devices below 65 decibels. A rough reference point is that normal conversation is around 60 decibels. You want the sound loud enough to mask disruptions but not so loud that it becomes the disruption itself.

**Q: Is white noise or brown noise better for babies?**
White noise is more commonly used and studied for infants, but the American Academy of Pediatrics advises keeping any sound machine at least 7 feet from the baby's sleep area and below 50 decibels. Brown noise is not well-studied in infants specifically. Always check with your pediatrician before using sleep sounds regularly with young children.

## Final Recommendation

If you're starting from scratch in 2026, try brown noise first. It suits a wider range of adult sleepers, feels less fatiguing over long nights, and does the same masking job as white noise for most home environments. If you sleep somewhere extremely noisy, or if you've genuinely tried brown noise and found it unhelpful, white noise is a solid fallback.

Either way, test both using a free player before buying hardware, keep the volume reasonable, and pair whatever you choose with a consistent sleep schedule. The sound is a tool, not a fix on its own.
