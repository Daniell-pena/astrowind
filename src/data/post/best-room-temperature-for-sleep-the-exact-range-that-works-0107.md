# Best Room Temperature for Sleep: The Exact Range That Works (2026 Guide)

You probably already know that your bedroom affects how well you sleep. But temperature is the one variable that most people set once and never think about again. That is a mistake. Getting your room to the right temperature is one of the fastest, cheapest changes you can make to improve your sleep quality tonight, not six weeks from now.

**Short answer:** The best room temperature for sleep is between 60 and 67 degrees Fahrenheit (15.6 to 19.4 degrees Celsius) for most adults. This range supports your body's natural drop in core temperature at sleep onset. Cooler is generally better than warmer, and temperatures above 72°F consistently disrupt sleep quality across most age groups, according to the Sleep Foundation.

---

## Why Does Room Temperature Actually Affect Sleep?

Your body does something specific as you get ready to sleep: it pushes heat toward your hands and feet to release it, which drops your core temperature by about 1 to 2 degrees Fahrenheit. This drop is a biological signal that tells your brain it is time to produce melatonin and move into deeper sleep stages.

If your room is too warm, that heat-release process gets blocked. Your body struggles to cool down, you spend more time in lighter sleep stages, and you wake up more often. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) consistently points to thermal discomfort as one of the leading environmental causes of poor sleep.

The opposite problem - a room that is too cold - can also disrupt sleep, though most people encounter the too-warm problem far more often. Extreme cold below 54°F can trigger wakefulness and muscle tension that fragments sleep.

The takeaway is simple. Your bedroom temperature is not just about comfort. It is an active part of your sleep biology. Treat it that way.

---

## What Is the Best Temperature for Deep Sleep Specifically?

Deep sleep, also called slow-wave sleep, is where your body repairs tissue, consolidates memory, and resets the immune system. It is the most physically restorative stage you go through each night.

Research referenced by Harvard Health suggests that deep sleep is especially sensitive to thermal environment. Slightly cooler rooms, toward the lower end of the 60 to 67°F range, tend to increase the proportion of time spent in slow-wave sleep. This is why people who sleep in warmer climates or overly heated rooms often report feeling unrefreshed even after a full night.

If deep sleep is a priority for you, aim closer to 65°F rather than 67°F. A smart thermostat or a programmable one that drops a degree or two an hour before your target bedtime can help you hit this window without thinking about it every night.

## Does the Best Sleep Temperature Change for Babies, Older Adults, or Women?

Yes, the ideal range shifts slightly depending on who is sleeping.

**Babies and toddlers** cannot regulate body temperature as efficiently as adults. Pediatric guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends keeping nurseries between 68 and 72°F. Cooler than that can be too cold for infants, and warmer increases overheating risk.

**Older adults** often feel cold more easily due to changes in metabolism and circulation. A range of 66 to 70°F tends to be more comfortable, while still being cool enough to support sleep quality. Layered bedding that can be adjusted mid-night works better than a fixed thermostat setting for this group.

**Women experiencing perimenopause or menopause** are a specific case. Hot flashes can spike body temperature dramatically and unpredictably during the night. Keeping the room cooler, at or below 65°F, and using moisture-wicking or cooling bedding can reduce the impact. Cooling mattress pads designed for this are worth looking at. (This is not medical advice. If hot flashes are severely disrupting your sleep, talk to your doctor about treatment options.)

**Athletes and people who train hard** often run warmer due to lingering metabolic heat. They frequently report better sleep at 63 to 65°F compared to the general population average.

## Simple Habits That Support the Right Sleep Temperature

You do not have to spend money to start improving your sleep temperature tonight. A few habit changes help a lot.

- Take a warm shower or bath 60 to 90 minutes before bed. This sounds backwards, but the heat brings blood to the skin surface and accelerates the core temperature drop afterward.
- Avoid heavy meals and intense exercise within two hours of bed. Both raise your metabolic heat significantly.
- Switch off ceiling lights earlier. Incandescent and halogen bulbs radiate more heat than LED alternatives.
- Open windows strategically. If outdoor temperatures drop in the evening, cross-ventilate by opening windows on opposite sides of your home for 20 to 30 minutes.
- Use breathable sleepwear or sleep with less on. What you wear to bed can trap heat as much as a too-warm room.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the best room temperature for sleep for adults?**
The ideal range for most adults is 60 to 67 degrees Fahrenheit (15.6 to 19.4 degrees Celsius). The Sleep Foundation and AASM both reference this range. Most people find 65°F to be the practical sweet spot.

**Q: Is 70 degrees too warm to sleep in?**
For most adults, yes, 70°F is on the high end and will reduce sleep quality for many people. It is not dramatically harmful, but you are likely to spend less time in deep sleep and wake up more often than you would at 65°F. Older adults and young children may tolerate 70°F better than younger adults.

**Q: Does sleeping in a cold room help you lose weight?**
There is some research suggesting that cooler sleep temperatures activate brown fat, which burns more calories. However, the effect is modest and should not be treated as a weight-loss strategy on its own. The primary reason to sleep cool is sleep quality, not calorie burning.

**Q: What if my partner and I prefer different sleep temperatures?**
This is one of the most common bedroom complaints. Dual-zone cooling mattress pads like the **Eight Sleep Pod Cover** let each side of the bed run at a different temperature. Alternatively, using separate blankets with different weights is a lower-cost approach that many couples find works well enough.

**Q: Can room temperature affect how long it takes to fall asleep?**
Yes. Sleep onset is closely tied to your body's core temperature drop. A room that is too warm slows that process and increases sleep latency, meaning it takes longer to fall asleep. Cooling your room to the 60 to 67°F range before bed is one of the most direct ways to fall asleep faster.

## Final Recommendation

Start at 65°F and adjust from there. If you sleep hot, aim lower. If you wake up cold in the night, add a layer rather than raising the thermostat. A programmable thermostat is the highest-return investment if you have compatible HVAC. If you do not, cooling sheets and a fan are a solid starting point. Track a few nights at different temperatures using a sleep tracker to find your personal sweet spot. Small adjustments to bedroom temperature consistently outperform supplements and gadgets for most people chasing better sleep.
